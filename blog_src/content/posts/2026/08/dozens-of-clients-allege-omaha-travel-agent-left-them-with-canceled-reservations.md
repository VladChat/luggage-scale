## Verify Before You Pay

Good agents remove friction. Great agents welcome verification.

Start with identity. Does the business have:
- A physical address you can verify.
- A domain email (not free webmail for contracts and invoices).
- A state business registration you can search.
- A consistent legal name across invoice, website, and bank info.

H3: Credentials that actually matter
- Industry affiliations can help. Look for ASTA, IATA, or CLIA numbers. Not every pro belongs, but active memberships raise confidence.
- Ask who will issue the ticket. A consolidator? The airline? The answer should be specific.
- Request a hold with a record locator. Airlines can create a booking hold before ticketing. That code lets you check details directly.

H3: Questions that get real answers
- Can you send the airline or hotel confirmation number, not just your internal invoice?
- When will e-tickets be issued, and what is the 13-digit ticket number format?
- Which fare rules apply? What are the change and refund penalties?
- How do I contact the supplier directly if there’s a disruption?

If answers are vague, slow down. Ask for an itemized invoice. Confirm that taxes, bags, and seats are included if advertised. If anything feels off, pause payment. A real professional will understand.

## Smart Ways to Pay

How you pay is as important as who you pay.

- Prefer credit cards. They give you dispute rights and timelines.
- Avoid wire transfers, Zelle, cash apps, and gift cards. These are hard to reverse.
- Don’t pay large balances far in advance without supplier confirmation.
- Look at the merchant name on your card statement. Does it match the business?
- Get an itemized invoice before any charge. Save PDFs and emails in one folder.

H3: Use your leverage
- Split payments. Pay deposits on card, then the balance after you confirm supplier receipts.
- Consider paying the airline or hotel directly when possible. Some agents can still service the booking.
- If a vendor refuses cards “to save fees,” be cautious. Fees are a cost of doing business.

H3: Chargeback timing that works
- Call your card issuer as soon as you suspect fraud or non-delivery.
- Provide the invoice, emails, and screenshots of failed confirmations.
- Document attempts to resolve with the agency. Keep everything in writing.
- Many issuers set strict windows, often 60–120 days from charge or service date. Act fast.

When a company knows you’ll use the protections you have, they tend to behave better. And if they don’t, you’re not stuck.

## Lock Down Your Reservations

Trust, but verify. It’s not rude. It’s smart.

H3: Confirm with the airline
- Ask for the airline’s record locator (six characters).
- Go to “Manage Booking” on the airline site and enter your last name and code.
- Look for 13-digit ticket numbers under each traveler. A real ticket shows two numbers starting with the airline’s code (e.g., 001 for American).
- If you only see seats but no e-ticket, it’s not complete. Politely insist on ticket issuance and proof.

H3: Confirm with the hotel
- Get the hotel’s native confirmation number, not the agency’s invoice number.
- Call the property. Ask them to read back the dates, room type, and rate.
- Request an email directly from the property. Save it.

H3: Confirm with car rentals and tours
- For rentals, request the confirmation and the rate code. Validate included insurance if abroad.
- For tours or activities, ask for vouchers with a local contact phone number.
- If a supplier says “we don’t see your booking,” stop any further payments immediately.

H3: Build your paper trail
- Keep a single email thread per trip with subject tags like “Trip 2026 – Flights – Hotel.”
- Save PDFs and screenshots. Include date stamps in filenames.
- Share a read-only folder with your travel companions.

These aren’t paranoia steps. They’re routine for frequent travelers. Once you do them twice, they take minutes and prevent hours of drama.

## Build a Personal Preflight Ritual

Rituals reduce stress. They also catch problems early.

Try this one-week-out checklist:
1. Open each reservation and screenshot key pages.
2. Confirm e-ticket numbers and seat assignments.
3. Recheck hotel night count and cancellation windows.
4. Add airline and hotel apps with your bookings loaded.
5. Verify passports and visas if crossing borders.
6. Check baggage rules for your fare class. Note weight limits.
7. Print essentials. A single folder still saves the day when phones die.

Day-before checklist:
- Reconfirm flight times. Check for overnight schedule changes.
- Revisit weather at departure and arrival cities.
- Recheck ground transfers or parking.
- Put essential meds and chargers in your personal item.
- Sleep. You’ll travel better with a calm mind.

H3: A simple communication plan
- Choose a primary and backup contact method with your agent or group.
- Share your live flight status link with a trusted person.
- Decide the “go/no-go” decision time if confirmations are still missing.

When plans wobble, your ritual guides you. You react faster and spend less.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q: What’s the fastest way to tell if my flight is truly ticketed?
A: Look for the 13-digit e-ticket number under each traveler in the airline’s “Manage Booking” page. A reservation code alone is not enough.

Q: I paid an agency by bank transfer and my reservation is canceled. What now?
A: Collect all documents and contact your bank immediately. File a police report if fraud is suspected, and contact your state consumer protection office. If any part was on a credit card, start a dispute right away.

Q: Can I verify a hotel reservation without going through the agent?
A: Yes. Call the property directly with your name and dates. Ask for the hotel’s own confirmation number and an email from their system.

Q: Are luggage scales allowed in carry-on bags?
A: Yes. Both digital and mechanical scales are allowed in carry-ons in most regions. If in doubt, check your airport’s security guidelines.

Q: Are mechanical luggage scales accurate enough?
A: A good model is accurate within a pound or two. Zero it before use, lift steadily, and leave a small buffer under the airline limit.
