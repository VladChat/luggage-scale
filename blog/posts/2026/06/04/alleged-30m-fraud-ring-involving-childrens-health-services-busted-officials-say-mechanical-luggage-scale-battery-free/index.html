The Case That Sparked This Guide

Federal authorities say a network of individuals and clinics billed for children’s behavioral health services that families never received. The figure—tens of millions—stops you cold. It also fits a pattern we’ve seen in health fraud: small claims multiplied across time, masked by confusing codes and the assumption that parents are too tired or busy to notice.

The details matter to prosecutors. For families, the lesson is sharper. Ghost services often hide where systems are opaque: outpatient therapy, home visits, telehealth check‑ins. When documentation is fuzzy, it’s easier for bad actors to invent appointments and “upcode” complexity.

If you’ve ever opened an Explanation of Benefits (EOB) and wondered, “Wait, what is this?” you’ve felt the weak link. Fraud relies on that moment—the shrug that moves a questionable charge into the “deal with it later” pile. Travel only increases that delay.

How Health Billing Scams Hit Travelers

Let’s connect the dots to the road. Families move more than ever. We do telehealth from airports, urgent care in ski towns, and therapy catch‑ups between tournaments. Those shifts expand access—and expand attack surface.

Here’s where trouble often starts:

  • Pop‑up or transient clinics in tourist zones.
  • Out‑of‑network providers that reassure “we’ll bill your plan anyway.”
  • Telehealth visits scheduled by third parties, then billed as longer, higher‑complexity care.
  • “Free screenings” that capture insurance details and later spawn mystery claims.

Authorities described a scheme built on claims for services never rendered. According to a CBS News report, investigators say families found charges for care that didn’t happen. The specifics will play out in court. The takeaway for travelers is immediate: if you cannot trace a visit to a date, a clinician, and notes, it’s vulnerable.

Travel adds noise. Jet lag blurs dates. Boarding times push calls to tomorrow. We toss receipts into pockets, switch SIM cards, and forget to download an invoice before a clinic’s portal locks us out. Scammers count on that chaos.

Five Steps to Protect Your Family

You don’t need a law degree. You need a repeatable process. Start with these five steps every trip:

  1. Confirm the provider before you go.
  • Call the number on your insurance card.
  • Ask: “Is this clinician in network for our plan? What is the NPI?” Write both down.
  • If out of network, ask how claims will be submitted and what you owe at point of service.
  1. Get proof for every encounter.
  • Ask for a visit summary before leaving the clinic.
  • Photograph the clinician’s name badge and office signage if appropriate.
  • Save receipts and any prior authorization reference numbers.
  1. Build a simple care log.
  • One note per visit: date, time, provider, what was done, any follow‑ups.
  • Add photos of business cards and invoices.
  • Sync it to the cloud before you change hotels or SIMs.
  1. Audit your EOBs within 30 days.
  • Match each line to your care log.
  • Flag mismatches: wrong date, wrong location, services that don’t make sense.
  • Dispute quickly: your card’s back number, then the insurer’s fraud line if needed.
  1. Lock down your child’s medical identity.
  • Ask your plan about alerts for new claims or out‑of‑network services.
  • Limit how and where you share insurance images.
  • Clear old documents from shared devices before lending them.

Pro tip: Do the verification call while you’re still packing. Ten quiet minutes at home can save two hours of airport‑gate panic.

Red Flags Before, During, and After a Visit

Fraud isn’t always obvious. These cues help you slow down and double‑check.

Before the visit

  • A clinic finds you, not the other way around—especially via social media or unsolicited calls.
  • The provider can’t confirm network status or dodges NPI and license questions.
  • You’re promised “no out‑of‑pocket cost,” regardless of your plan.

During the visit

  • No intake paperwork, no vitals, and no private interaction between clinician and child.
  • You’re asked to sign blank forms or leave insurance cards “for later.”
  • The encounter is oddly brief for the billed service.

After the visit

  • You can’t access a portal or notes for what was allegedly done.
  • The invoice uses vague service descriptions or mismatched CPT codes.
  • New claims appear for places you never visited or times you weren’t in town.

When in doubt, pause. Ask for the visit summary. Get names spelled out. If something feels off, step outside and call your insurer from the parking lot.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q: How can I spot billing for services we never received? A: Keep a simple care log. Match each Explanation of Benefits (EOB) line to a date, provider, and service in your notes. Flag mismatches or vague descriptions. Call your insurer’s number on the card and ask them to open a review.

Q: What should I do if a clinic pressures me to sign blank forms? A: Don’t sign. Ask for completed forms and copies before you leave. If they refuse, consider finding another provider and report the incident to your insurer and, if appropriate, your state’s consumer protection office.

Q: How do I verify a provider while traveling? A: Call your insurer to check network status and request the provider’s NPI and license. Cross‑check on the state licensing board website when possible, and ask the clinic to print a visit summary before you go.

Q: Is telehealth safer than walk‑in clinics for travelers? A: Both can be fine. Telehealth through your insurer or established systems offers clearer records and billing. Walk‑ins are useful for urgent needs—just verify network status, keep paperwork, and pay attention to red flags.

Q: What’s the simplest way to avoid surprise baggage fees? A: Weigh your bag the night before. Leave a small buffer under your airline’s limit, and re‑check before returning home. A reliable, non‑digital travel scale helps you adjust before you reach the counter.
